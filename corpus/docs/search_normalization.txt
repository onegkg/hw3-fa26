Search Normalization: Design Decisions and Trade-offs

Text normalization for search engines involves a series of decisions that
collectively determine what counts as "the same" for retrieval purposes.

Common normalization steps:
- Lowercasing: "Python" and "python" match the same documents
- Accent folding: "résumé" and "resume" treated equivalently
- Punctuation stripping: "node.js" may become "node" or "nodejs"
- Hyphen handling: "co-op" may become "coop", "co op", or stay "co-op"
- Stopword removal: "the", "a", "in" may be dropped entirely
- Stemming or lemmatization: "running" → "run" (not used here)

Each decision affects recall (finding relevant documents) and precision
(avoiding irrelevant ones). Aggressive normalization increases recall at
the cost of precision; conservative normalization does the reverse.

Human-centered correctness:
"Correctness" in search is not purely a technical question. It depends on
what users expect and what distinctions they care about.

- A user who types "résumé" probably wants results about résumés, whether
  documents use the accented or unaccented form. Accent folding helps here.
- A user who searches for "naive" may or may not want results for "naïve".
  In technical writing, the distinction can matter.
- A user who types "co-op" probably does not want results about chicken coops.
  Hyphen removal that collapses "co-op" into "coop" introduces noise.
- A user who searches for "The Who" has no way to find results if the system
  strips both "the" and "who" as stopwords.

A key principle: whatever normalization is applied to documents at index time
must also be applied to queries at search time. Inconsistency between these
two normalization paths is a common and subtle source of bugs.
